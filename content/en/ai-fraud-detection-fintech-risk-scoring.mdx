Financial fraud almost never looks like what people imagine. It's rarely one obviously suspicious transaction with a huge amount sent to an unusual country. More often, it's a sequence of small actions that each look perfectly normal on their own — but together form a pattern that only a system capable of analyzing thousands of signals at once can catch.

That's exactly the job a well-built AI risk scoring system does.

## Why fixed rules stopped being enough

Fraud detection used to rely on hand-written rules: block any transaction above a certain amount, flag logins from a new country, cap the number of transactions per hour. The problem is that these rules are static, and fraudsters adapt fast — once a rule becomes known, it becomes easy to route around. That's the same realization that pushed teams like [Galylio](https://galylio.com/about) toward building models specific to each business rather than systems based on fixed rule sets.

A machine learning model trained on actual user behavior works differently: it doesn't apply a checklist of rules, it learns what "normal" looks like for each individual profile, then flags meaningful deviations. A customer who usually makes small online purchases in the evening and suddenly initiates several large transfers at 4am triggers a signal — not because an explicit rule forbids it, but because that behavior sharply deviates from their own history.

## What separates a good system from a generic one

The quality of a fraud detection model depends almost entirely on the quality and specificity of the data it's trained on. A model trained on generic data, not specific to the financial institution or the local market, generates a flood of false positives — legitimate transactions wrongly blocked — or worse, misses fraud patterns specific to the local context.

That's the principle behind [Galylio's approach](https://galylio.com/services) to fintech use cases: a model built on the institution's own real data, trained to understand its actual customers, its own transaction patterns, and its own regulatory context — rather than a generic off-the-shelf system applied without adaptation.

## The real cost of false positives

An often-underestimated point: an overly aggressive fraud detection system is expensive, even if it never lets a single fraudulent transaction through. Every legitimate transaction wrongly blocked creates friction, frustrates the customer, and in the worst cases, pushes that customer to switch banks or payment providers entirely.

The challenge isn't just detecting fraud — it's doing so with enough precision to avoid penalizing honest users. That's a hard balance to strike with fixed rules, and it's exactly where a model trained on specific data makes the difference — it learns to distinguish an unusual-but-legitimate purchase (a one-off large expense for a family event, say) from genuinely suspicious behavior.

## Healthcare, insurance, retail: the same logic applies elsewhere

The principle of scoring based on deviation from normal behavior isn't limited to banking fraud. The same logic shows up in healthcare case triage, where a system can flag cases that deviate from typical patterns for faster prioritization, or in insurance risk assessment. These are [sector-specific use cases](https://galylio.com/services) that share the same underlying architecture: a model that learns what's normal in order to detect what isn't.

## What a financial institution should check before choosing a system

Before adopting an AI fraud detection system, a few questions separate a real technical partner from a generic vendor:

- **Is the model trained on your actual data**, or on a generic dataset not specific to your market?
- **Who has access to your transaction data**, and is it shared with other clients of the same vendor?
- **How does the system evolve over time** as new fraud patterns emerge?
- **What's the real false-positive rate**, not just the advertised detection rate?

In an industry where customer trust is the most valuable asset, a poorly calibrated fraud detection system can do more damage than the fraud it's meant to prevent.
